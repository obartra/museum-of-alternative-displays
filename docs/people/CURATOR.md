# Curator's readings of the accounts of people

## 22 November 2026: the first thirteen

Read together: Kenyon Taylor, Ferdinand Braun, Robert H. Anderson, Donald Bitzer, Gene Slottow, Robert Willson,
Nicholas Sheridon, Martin Schadt, Wolfgang Helfrich, Fritz Fischer, Joseph Ostoja-Kotkowski, Joseph Plateau,
Gabriel Lippmann.

Changed in the reading:

- **Bitzer, Slottow, Willson.** The three accounts told the making of the plasma panel in three different orders
  of names. Each now names the other two the same way, and the Emmy Award of 2002 is "received" in all three.
- **Anderson.** "The first instrument built around it" claimed a first that the source does not vouch for in
  those words. It now says the Type 564 of 1963 was built around the tube.
- **Sheridon.** The year he thought of Gyricon is left out: an interview with him gives 1974, and MIT Technology
  Review has him joining the laboratory in 1975. The account keeps to what both agree on.
- **Schadt and Helfrich.** Both accounts rest on Schadt's own telling of 1970. Helfrich's says so by keeping to
  the two facts that telling gives about him.
- **Plateau, Ostoja-Kotkowski.** Each "first" is given to the institution that says it.
- **Taylor.** One sentence only: the patent is the one source that is not an encyclopedia. Left short on purpose.

## 22 November 2026, later: 127 more

Ten researchers each took thirteen people, with the brief in this folder's voice and the same checks the museum
runs. They wrote 127 accounts. Three people have none, because nothing beyond an encyclopedia was found: Mozi,
Auguste Berthier and Kazuo Yoshinaka. All 140 accounts were then read together.

Changed in the reading:

- **Health, belief and family left out** where a researcher had kept them: why Kepler left Graz, what Ibn
  al-Haytham turned away from, why Latimer retired, Wheatstone's father, Elma Farnsworth's age.
- **González Camarena.** The sentence on how he died is gone: the sources tell it differently.
- **Welch.** A count of citations is not a fact about a person. Removed.
- **Smoot.** One sentence said less than it seemed to. Removed.
- **Brus, Sadashiva.** Years of death moved out of the prose where the plate can carry them.

What the reading could not do: open each source again. Every account names its sources, and the researchers kept
a short quotation for every paragraph, but some of those came from summaries of a page and not its own words.

## 22 November 2026, evening: the adversarial review

Before the accounts were translated, the owner asked that they be attacked: for the museum's voice, for historical
accuracy, and with care for and emphasis of marginalized groups and communities. Twelve reviewers took the 140
accounts, each told to assume a sentence was wrong until it had shown otherwise and to give evidence for every
finding. Seven editors then decided every finding, group by group, and the curator read every changed paragraph.

**The reviewers**

- **Accuracy** (seven, twenty accounts each): every statement tested against the pages the account cites. All 396
  paragraphs have a verdict: 336 supported, 48 partly, 9 contradicted by their own source, 3 whose source could
  not be read.
- **Voice**: against `VOICE.md` and the labels themselves.
- **Care**, in two parts. One read every woman, everyone on the blue line, everyone the accounts place outside
  Europe and North America, and the communities the labels name, against their own sources: is the account thinner
  than those beside it, is the work told as their own, is a documented barrier or a withheld credit left out. The
  other read all the language: who is defined through someone else, whose work is called help, what the passive
  hides, how names and places outside Europe are written, and whether everyone is held to one standard of proof.
- **Consistency**: accounts against each other and against the labels, and every "first" and "only".
- **Reader**: a visitor of fifteen, then nine translators.

They raised 367 findings: 31 "must", 246 "should", 90 "consider". The editors accepted 149, adapted 177, declined 8
and deferred 14. 122 of the 140 accounts changed. Three people who had no account now have one: Mozi, Kazuo
Yoshinaka and Auguste Berthier.

**What changed most**

- **Claims that went a step past their source.** A prize given "for femto-photography" that was given for a body
  of work. "Founded" where the source says co-founded. A patent count that was only part of the count. A "recalled"
  that was the museum's source speaking, not the person. A Latin title that belongs to a later printed edition.
- **Shared work told as one person's.** Co-inventors, co-authors and co-founders now get the same verb in every
  account that tells the deed: the plasma panel, the twisted nematic effect, electrophoretic ink, the disc-shaped
  liquid crystals of Bangalore, the microLED, the quantum-dot diode of 1994, Laser Images, Pepper's ghost.
- **Work told through someone else.** Elma Farnsworth's account now leads with what she did in the laboratory.
  Marie Van Brittan Brown files her own patent in the active voice. Jingyu Lin's account has her share of the
  microLED. Elsa Garmire is a founder of Laser Images in Ivan Dryer's account as well as her own. Mary Lou Jepsen's
  account names her thesis on the holographic video display, and Stephen Benton's says he supervised it.
- **Barriers and withheld credit that the sources record, and the accounts had left out.** Lewis Latimer's parents'
  escape from enslavement, his lower pay, the credit Hiram Maxim took, the workers who resented a Black supervisor.
  The unwritten policy at Sears that first kept Charles Harrison off its staff. The school that did not encourage
  Valerie Thomas. Margaret Benyon making holograms when holography was open only to scientists. Each is stated
  plainly, with the source named as witness. None is an adjective.
- **Who the work was for.** The Tretiakoffs' accounts now say, in their patent's words, what braille books cost
  blind readers. Kara Walker's and Hank Willis Thomas's accounts now say what the work is about.
- **Accounts that were a list of posts.** Each now says what the person did that bears on the piece: Hagelbarger,
  Sproull, Macfarlane, Hammond, Akinwande, Borden, Pighini, Rozin, Welch, Follmer, Kenyon Taylor.
- **Disagreements between sources, named and not settled.** Fergason's discovery (1967 or 1969), Heilmeier at
  DARPA (1974 or 1975), Goldmark's colour system ("never commercially used", or broadcast for four months in 1951),
  Spindt's cathode (1966 or 1973), Barbaro's birth and his book, Elma Farnsworth's birth, the Latin translation of
  Ibn al-Haytham's Optics.

**Findings that were wrong.** Eight were declined. Four had misread a source the editor then read again: the ACM
does call Sutherland's headset the first of its kind, Vicki Colvin was a professor of chemistry at Rice, Mark
Miles's biography does give the sale to Qualcomm, and Gerardo Beni's page does carry the journal he co-founded.

**The curator's own changes on top of the editors'**, each for a stated reason: the Institute for Soldier
Nanotechnologies in the plural, as MIT writes it. Valerie Thomas's first paragraph, to keep to what her source says
of her school. Jepsen's thesis and Benton's supervision of it, from the MIT record. Lippmann's years, from the Nobel
Foundation. Ibn Daniyal's play titles: one in the form a scholarly source prints, two by the senses the puppetry
encyclopedia gives. A paragraph on Ives that set two sources against each other over nothing. Atalla's account, so
that he and Kahng share the verb for the transistor. Jingyu Lin's funding sentence, to say no more than the source.
Ibn al-Haytham's plate, emptied because both his years are "about" and the plate cannot say so.

**Also decided**

- **W. von Jaworski.** No source gives a first name. The page had "Werner". It now has the initial.
- **John Ralston.** His portrait and outside link are withdrawn. The study photograph is of the founder of
  Neursantys, and no source read ties that man to the author at SDL in 1996.
- **Samuel Achilefu.** A reviewer half remembered a report of his death. A search found none.
- **The Nixie tube.** The curator's first correction of the label said no record tied Hagelbarger to the tube.
  That was wrong. The Computer Museum's report of winter 1987 to 1988 lists the prototype as his gift and calls him
  its inventor, and the Computer History Museum holds it. The same museum's catalogue says the Nixie was invented at
  Burroughs. The label and his account now give both.

**Still open**

- The year of death of Andrew Haeff (an encyclopedia gives 1990, and his birth as 1904 or 1905 by the calendar
  used) and of Charles Spindt (his obituary says he died at 92). Both plates show a birth year alone.
- Whether a court or the Patent Office ruled for Farnsworth in 1935. Plateau's priority against Stampfer. The
  later history of Schönherr's firm. Kahng's work of 1968 on zinc sulphide films, which the label cites and no
  source read carries.
- Names in Japanese, Chinese and Korean script. The museum prints romanised names in the order the people publish
  under. Translators keep the Latin script unless an established form exists. A list of names in their own script,
  each from the person's own institution, is still to be made.
- For translators: no source states the sex of A. Frankenstein or of M. C. Schlamp. Patent numbers are fixed strings.
  A named chair keeps its name and has its rank translated.
- The labels. The review showed that many label lines claim more than any cited source carries. An audit of 61
  lines followed the same evening, and its record is in the next entry.

The reviewers' findings, the verdict on every paragraph and each editor's decisions are kept outside the
repository with the research notes.

## 22 November 2026, night: the labels audited

The review of the accounts showed that many label lines said more than any cited page carries. Three auditors then
took 61 lines, the ones the reviewers and editors had marked, and tested each against the sources on the piece and
any they could find. Their verdicts: 10 stand, 40 are true and needed rewording, 8 differ from their source, and 3
had no source at all. The curator decided each line. 51 lines changed, with 26 sentences in the piece texts that
said the same things, and about 80 sources were added to 31 pieces.

**What the labels no longer say on their own authority**

- **Firsts.** A first now stands only with its witness. Britannica calls Braun's tube the first oscilloscope.
  California Historical Landmark 941 calls Farnsworth's picture the first all-electronic television image. The ACM
  calls Sutherland's headset the first of its kind and the Computer History Museum calls it an early one, and the
  label gives both. MIT News calls Javan's laser the first gas laser. Gyricon, the Eidophor, Tang and Van Slyke's
  diode, the microLED of Kansas State and the holographic video display of 1989 are told by what was made and when.
- **Dates that were one source's, or nobody's.** The flip-disc is dated by its patent application of 1960. The
  Trans-Canada Air Lines story is gone, because only a Wikipedia article and a collector's site carry it, and they
  disagree. The Eidophor prototype ran in 1944 by an account of 1953, not in 1943. Kepler's 1604 is gone, and the
  1911 Britannica is named for his use of the words camera obscura. Séraphin's 1771 is gone, and Britannica's 1776
  for Paris is set beside the move of 1784. Gabor conceived holography in 1947 by his Nobel lecture and published
  it in 1948. The microLED gave light in August 1999 by its makers' account and was published in 2000. Nine pieces
  carry a line under the year that says which kind of date it is.
- **Deeds that were someone else's.** Elma Farnsworth's line now says what she did in the laboratory. Jepsen's
  thesis and Benton's supervision of it are one sentence. Hammond supervised and DeLongchamp built. Jiang and Lin
  are two physicists, not a married couple. Sheridon's line on the flexible display piece is now an aside, because
  the board that drove Gyricon was rigid.
- **Birthplaces.** A birthplace stays where a reliable source gives it: Hong Kong, Seoul, Port Said, Tehran,
  Nigeria, Paris. Three that no source read carries are gone: Florence, England and Jerusalem. `VOICE.md` now
  says so.

**Still open on the labels**

- The Trans-Canada Air Lines commission for the flip-disc, and whether the airline was by then Air Canada.
- Schönherr's braille element of 1975: whether it was piezoelectric, and the later history of his firm.
- The text of German patent 172 376, which the museum cites from two histories and has not read.
- The witness for Fergason's settlement with Kent State, which is so far a preprint.
- A source for the name Mohamed Atalla. The Hewlett-Packard Journal calls him John.
- Whether the split-flap board at Liège in 1956 is the earliest on record, given a mention of Solari numerals at
  Florence in the 1930s.
- Whether Naish belongs on the head-up display piece as an aside.
- One source on the Pepper's ghost piece, a Westminster archive page, does not mention the ghost.

## 22 November 2026, night: nine translations of the reviewed text

The 472 paragraphs of the accounts and 91 label strings went to nine translators after the review, not before. The
brief told each to keep every named witness, to keep each person as the subject of their own deed, and to carry
over no claim the English had dropped. The curator read their reports and ruled on what they raised.

- **Words for people.** Each language uses its current respectful terms in the museum's own sentences: persones
  esclavitzades, personas esclavizadas, pessoas escravizadas, versklavte Menschen, 被奴役的人.
- **A work is described as it is.** The story of Kara Walker's film Testimony is "masters and slaves" in every
  language, in the Walker Art Center's words. Five translators had written enslaved people there, and the curator
  had changed the English to match. The owner asked that the description of the work be left unaltered, and it
  is. `VOICE.md` now says so.
- **"Person of colour"**, the Science History Institute's words about Paula Hammond, is persona no blanca in
  Catalan and Spanish, pessoa não branca in Portuguese, personne non blanche in French, Person of Color in German
  and 有色人种 in Chinese. Three translators had first written racialitzada, racializada and racisée. The owner,
  a Catalan speaker, pointed out that those words say a person is marked by racism, which is not what the
  source says. Readers who know a better term are asked to say so.
- **No sex is stated** for A. Frankenstein, M. C. Schlamp, Kazuo Yoshinaka or Berthier in any language. The name,
  the patent or the listing carries the sentence.
- **A sentence the English left open.** Pepper's account said the patent named both men "at his request". Three
  translators took the request to be Dircks's. Pepper's book says "at my request". The English now says "at his
  own request", and all nine languages say the same. A check then looked for any other paragraph where a
  translation names someone the English paragraph does not, and found none.
- **Cities take the name the language uses** (Lovaina, Löwen, Louvain, Estrasburgo, Straßburg). Institutions keep
  their own names.
- **Names in other scripts.** Chinese gives the established Chinese names of people who have one (邓青云, 彭笑刚,
  中村修二). Japanese keeps the kanji the earlier pages used and writes no given name in kanji that a source has
  not shown: a translator's reading of Tadashi Nakamura's given name was taken out. Korean writes Japanese names
  in Hangul by sound. Everyone else stays in Latin script.

## Ideas kept for later

Two guided lines were proposed and set aside for now: **The move east**, on how the making of screens moved from
American and European laboratories to Japan, then Korea, Taiwan and China, and **Before electricity**, from the
camera obscura and the shadow play to the spinning picture discs. No new lines are being added at present.
